# Decisions

Why lidar-game is built the way it is: each entry gives the choice, what else was considered, and what was
measured. The platform research behind the stack (WebXR on the Steam Frame, PC streaming, three.js, Rapier,
input, the emulator loop) is in `C:\Projects\cc\vr-test\DECISIONS.md` §0–12 and isn't repeated here.
`PLAN.md` has the plan and the reasoning about the video stream.

Written 2026-10-20 with the first milestone (V1, technical viability).

---

## 1. What Alex asked for (2026-10-20)

- **Tone:** tense exploration, no jump scares at all. Liminal, empty, lonely; eerie, colourless and soulless
  spaces. Simple environment puzzles maybe later, built on after this milestone; panels on pedestals don't fit
  this kind of game (the first test level had some; they're gone).
- **Movement:** analogue.
- **Levels:** authored in Blender; they must be intentional.
- **Scans persist.**
- **Dot colour:** by material and by distance, the distance part live (it changes as you move), not fixed
  when the dot was cast.
- **Entity materials** whose dots fade over time, for moving things.
- **First milestone:** the technical part, to see whether the project is viable.
- Public repo `alexh95/lidar-game`; working name lidar-game.

## 2. Stack: vr-test's foundation

The same stack as vr-test (WebXR + three.js r186 + Rapier 0.21 + TypeScript 7 + Vite 8), with its proven
pieces copied: the input layer, the IWER emulator shaped like the Frame, the hidden-page frame clock, the test
driver, the snapshot path with the sRGB fix, the hand model, the Web Audio engine, the wrist-menu pattern.
Everything playground-specific was left behind. Considered: a new engine. Not worth it: vr-test's input,
emulator and test loop were hard-won, and the dot renderer is a few hundred lines on top of three.

## 3. Levels from Blender

- **The `.blend` is the source; the game loads a `.glb`.** `npm run levels` runs Blender in the background
  with `tools/blender/export_glb.py`. Only what the game needs is exported: triangles (modifiers applied),
  material names, object names, custom properties and object animations. No UVs, normals or textures:
  the 3.4k-triangle test level is 161 KB.
- **Meaning comes from names** (`levels/README.md`): the material name picks the dot colour, an `entity…`
  material makes dots fade, `SPAWN` places the player, `BLOCK_` makes invisible walls, `X_` is left out.
  Considered: custom properties for everything. Names are visible in the outliner at a glance, which matters
  when you're authoring by hand; custom properties still come through for later use.
- **Animation is authored in Blender too.** Each action becomes a glTF animation, played looping by three's
  `AnimationMixer`; each moving mesh carries a kinematic Rapier trimesh that follows it every frame, so rays
  hit it where it is drawn. Verified by casting a ray at the turning monolith at five times in its cycle: the
  hit matches where the box surface is to the centimetre each time.
- **The first level was generated by a script** (`tools/blender/build_hall.py`) so it could be built without
  a hand in Blender, and it's meant to be edited by hand from now on. The script refuses to overwrite it.
- **Blender 5 detail:** a keyframe's interpolation can't be set through the old `action.fcurves` (removed in
  5.0) or by the preference in background mode (ignored). The script walks the layered action's channelbags.
  The first export had the monolith easing in and out every turn, which the collider test caught (15° at a
  quarter of its period).
- **A save is tied to the level file's content hash.** After you edit and re-export a level, old dots no
  longer match the walls, so a save from another version is dropped.

## 4. The scanner

- **Hold the trigger to scan; squeeze harder to focus.** The cone's half-angle goes from 17° at a light touch
  to 4.5° at full squeeze, so one control is both a wide wash and a detail spot. Considered: separate modes on
  buttons (sweep line, focus, pulse; in the plan). They can come later; one analogue control was enough to
  test the technology.
- **Rays are scattered at random over the cone.** The first version used a golden-angle spiral (even coverage
  per frame, turned randomly each frame): the accumulated cloud showed its arcs clearly (caught in a snapshot).
  Random directions with deduplication fill the surface without structure for the eye or the encoder to see.
- **Ray budget, not ray count.** Each frame casts as many rays as fit in 1.6 ms of main-thread time, from the
  measured cost per ray: 1.2–1.9 µs in the game (one reused `RAPIER.Ray`, no normals), so 850–1,300 rays a
  frame, about 60–95 k rays a second at 72 Hz.
- **Feedback:** a faint, steady cone of light from the lens that shows the spread (stable, so cheap for the
  video encoder, unlike per-ray beam lines that change every frame), a small lens light that lights the
  hands, a hum whose pitch follows the trigger, a hiss that follows the hit rate, and light haptics.

## 5. The point cloud

- **8 m chunks, each with a dedup grid and blocks of dots.** A hit whose 2.5 cm cell is taken is dropped, so
  the cloud grows with the area you scan, not the time you spend: rescanning a patch added 1 dot after the
  first pass added 179 (self-test `dedup`). Blocks are `THREE.Points` of up to 65,536 dots with the chunk's
  bounding sphere, so off-screen chunks are culled. New dots are uploaded as ranges (`addUpdateRange`): every
  frame uploads exactly what it added (self-test `upload-range`).
- **The dedup grid is shifted by half a cell.** Authored walls sit on round coordinates, which were exactly
  cell boundaries (and chunk boundaries every 8 m): the last bits of float noise put hits on both sides, so
  walls got two dots per cell. Saving to 32-bit floats then merged them, and a restore came back a third
  short. Found by the persistence test; the grid now has its boundaries halfway between multiples of the cell
  size, and keys are computed from the 32-bit position that's stored.
- **17 bytes a dot:** position and birth time as four floats, plus a material byte. Blocks double in size as
  they fill (4k to 64k dots), which keeps half-empty blocks cheap: 950k dots from a scanning tour take 31 MB.
  The plan's compact 8-byte format (16-bit chunk-local positions) is still available if memory becomes the
  limit.
- **A cap of 4 M dots** (measured to fit the GPU budget, §7). At the cap the cloud stops growing and says so
  in the menu. Considered: recycling the oldest blocks. Stopping is simpler and could become a gameplay
  element ("memory full"); Alex's call later.

## 6. The dots on screen

- **Colour = material palette × live distance shading.** A dot stores its material, not a colour. The shader
  looks the material up in a 256-entry palette texture and dims it by its current distance to the eye: full
  brightness inside 1.2 m, a log falloff to a floor of 30% at 30 m. Walk toward a wall and it brightens;
  brightness reads as depth. Palettes switch live with no re-upload: **Ash** (default: near-monochrome, the
  materials differ by a faint tint and brightness; entity dots are a pale warm white), **Tinted** (stronger
  hues, for comparison) and **Mono**.
- **Brightness carries the information, not hue.** That matches the colourless brief and the video stream:
  4:2:0 chroma is a quarter resolution, and small coloured dots lose their colour first (plan §6).
- **Size:** 1.1 cm across in the world, never under 2.5 eye-buffer pixels (adjustable: 1.5 / 2.5 / 3.5) nor
  over 12. New dots grow in over 0.16 s instead of popping.
- **Soft edges without blending:** alpha-to-coverage on the 4× MSAA buffer, so dots stay opaque (no sorting).
  Tone mapping is off everywhere, so a dot's brightness is exactly what the palette and the floor say.
- **Occlusion pass (on by default):** the level's triangles are drawn into depth only before the dots, so
  dots behind walls you haven't scanned stay hidden, as they would in the real space. The self-test scans a
  wall, walks round behind two others and finds 0 lit pixels (with x-ray on, 2% of the view). Dots are nudged
  a few millimetres toward the eye and the depth pass pushed back with polygon offset, so a dot always wins
  against the wall it lies on.
- **Entity dots** go into a separate ring of 65,536 that is never deduplicated or saved. Each dot stays where
  the ray hit, then dims and shrinks away over 2.6 s; the shader hides expired ones, so the ring is one draw
  call with no bookkeeping. A moving thing leaves a fading trail of where it was (`docs/monolith.jpg`).
- **Look so far:** the snapshots in `docs/` read as rooms. Heavily scanned surfaces far away (10 m+) fill in
  to near-solid grey at 2.5 px dots and 2.5 cm spacing; whether that's good (calmer, cheap to encode) or
  should stay dotty (a larger dedup cell with distance) is a question for the fidelity lab (plan §6.4).

## 7. Performance (measured on this PC, RTX 4080)

GPU time for two 2160×2160 eyes with 4× MSAA through the real renderer (`__vr.bench.eyes`, frames back to
back; the bench fills the level with random scans):

| Dots in the cloud | Worst view (x-ray, everything in view) | Normal views (occlusion on) |
|---|---|---|
| 1 M | 0.93–1.0 ms (2.0 M dot-draws for both eyes) | 0.55–0.65 ms |
| 2 M | 1.7 ms | 0.65–0.8 ms (in the hall) |
| 4 M | 2.8 ms (7.6 M dot-draws) | 1.0 ms (hall), 2.2 ms (terrace, looking over the expanse) |

- CPU time to submit both eyes: 0.4–0.9 ms (about 220–490 draw calls).
- Within the plan's budget (≤ 3 ms GPU for the dots). Without MSAA, 1 M dots cost 0.66 ms instead of 0.95.
- **How to measure:** an earlier version waited a frame between measured frames and got 1–5 ms for the same
  scene, varying from run to run: the GPU clocks down between frames. Rendering back to back (as a headset
  does) gave stable numbers that match the standalone benchmark (`bench/points-bench.html`, 0.42 ms per
  million in the same browser pane, as in the plan). In VR the GPU is busy every frame, so back-to-back is the
  realistic case.
- **Rays:** `bench/ray-bench.mjs` (now using this project's Rapier) gives 2.4–2.7 µs per ray at 5k–500k
  triangles, within 20% of the plan's numbers. In the game the scanner measures 1.2–1.9 µs per ray.
- **Persistence:** a save rewrites only the blocks that grew: 10 blocks (10.5k new dots) took 11 ms end to
  end, a full copy of 950k dots 49 ms. Restoring 950k dots takes about 200 ms, and the page is ready 0.56 s
  after load. Not yet measured: how much of a save blocks the main thread (each grown block is copied before
  it's stored). If it shows as a hitch every 5 s while scanning, spread the blocks over frames or move the
  copy to a worker.

## 8. Saving the scan

- **IndexedDB, one record per block,** under a slot and level key (`main/hall/<chunk>/<block>`), plus a meta
  record with the level hash. Every 5 s, blocks that gained dots are rewritten; everything unsaved is flushed
  when the page hides or the VR session ends. Birth times aren't kept: restored dots are fully grown.
- **Restores rebuild the same layout:** records come back in key order, block 000 first, so the restored
  blocks match the saved ones and later saves stay incremental. Verified by the self-test (every 97th dot
  compared after a restore) and by reloading the page with 950k dots.
- Considered: the File System Access API or a download. IndexedDB needs no permission prompt and survives
  reloads; an export/import file can come later for sharing or backups.
- The self-test saves into its own slot (`selftest`) and never touches the real one. `?slot=name` picks a slot,
  `?fresh` starts empty without loading.

## 9. Walking

- **Analogue stick walking with collision.** The body is a 1.3 m cylinder driven by Rapier's character
  controller: it slides along walls, follows slopes up to 42° and snaps to the ground going down. Speed follows
  how far the stick is pushed (1.6 m/s at full: deliberate, for tense exploration). Right stick turns: snap
  45° by default, 30° or smooth as options.
- **Your head leans freely; your body follows it.** The stick moves the body and you with it. When you move
  in the room, the body steps after your head once it's more than 30 cm away, and never off a ledge (it needs
  ground under its centre). The head itself is a 12 cm ball cast out from the body: it stops at a wall's
  surface and can't lean more than 75 cm past where the body can follow. Walking into an unscanned wall in
  the dark stops you whether you used the stick or your legs, and stepping back frees you at once.
- **Why (first playtest, 2026-10-20):** the first version kept the body exactly under the head, so leaning
  over the mezzanine railing or an edge pushed the whole view back: the body hit the railing, or would have
  walked off the edge. Now the self-test leans 70 cm out over the railing with no push at all, leans out over
  the stairs' open side without falling, and walks 2 m in the room with the body trailing 30 cm and nothing
  pushed back.
- **Steps are our own.** Rapier's autostep never climbed while ground snapping was on (it twitched 2 cm up at
  the first riser and stopped; measured). The controller now tries a blocked move again lifted by 28 cm,
  then settles; it keeps that only if it got further and landed no more than a step higher, so walls and
  benches still block. With a capsule the round bottom rolled back off each step's edge at walking speed; the
  flat-bottomed cylinder doesn't. The hall's 16 steps take 8.4 s at full stick (the same as the flat
  distance), and going down never leaves the ground.
- **Height follows smoothly:** each 25 cm step is a short glide (about 45 ms) rather than a pop.
- A comfort vignette closes in with stick speed (switchable).

## 10. Testing without a headset

The same loop as vr-test: `?emulate&selftest` runs **24 scripted checks** against the emulated Frame, each
driven the way a player would and checked with numbers: boot, scanning a wall (all dots on the wall, all
inside the cone), dedup, range uploads, save and restore, incremental saves, dropping a save from another
level version, entity dots fading, the entity collider following its animation, analogue walking speed,
wall collision, the head stopping at a wall, walking in the room, leaning over a railing and over a ledge,
the stairs, snap turning, opening the menu with the palm and clicking it
with the scanner (which mustn't fire), live settings, dots visible and dimmer far away, occlusion, the dot cap,
1 M dots within the GPU budget, and no frame errors. All 24 pass on the dev server and the production build.
